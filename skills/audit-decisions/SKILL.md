# 杉本式・判断監査

Treat a decision as an updateable operating hypothesis, not as a statement of identity. Preserve the objective; allow the means to change when conditions change.

## Select the audit mode

- Use **prospective audit** before a decision: compare options, downside, reversibility, and information value.
- Use **live audit** after a decision but before the outcome is known: test current evidence, counterevidence, and change conditions without reacting to noise.
- Use **retrospective audit** after outcomes appear: distinguish decision quality at the time from luck and later information.

## Run the audit

1. **State the true objective.** Identify what is being optimized and rank competing objectives. Detect when a proxy, number, rule, or method has replaced the real objective.
2. **Set the decision timestamp.** Use only information reasonably available at that time when judging the original decision. Label later information separately.
3. **Separate the record.** Classify inputs as observations, interpretations, assumptions, preferences, constraints, and unknowns. Do not present inference as fact.
4. **Reconstruct the current thesis.** Express the decision as a short causal claim: because A and B, choose X to improve Y, provided C does not occur.
5. **Steelman support.** Present the strongest evidence that makes the decision reasonable. Do not manufacture balance when the evidence is one-sided.
6. **Search for counterevidence.** Identify facts that weaken the causal claim, hidden correlations, alternative explanations, second-order effects, and signs that the operating regime has changed.
7. **Judge materiality.** Distinguish ordinary noise from evidence that reaches the decision's core. Counterevidence is a monitoring input, not an automatic veto.
8. **Compare alternatives by risk-adjusted value.** Consider expected benefit, plausible harm, irreversibility, opportunity cost, volatility or burden, recovery time, and the value of waiting. Do not rank options by maximum upside alone.
9. **Prefer the smallest informative action.** When possible, recommend a limited, reversible change that tests the causal hypothesis while protecting the primary objective.
10. **Define update conditions.** Specify what would strengthen, weaken, invalidate, pause, or reverse the decision; include a review horizon and the observations to collect.
11. **Separate authority.** State which choices belong to the user and which require a doctor, lawyer, fiduciary, employer, regulator, or other accountable expert. Never convert analysis into unauthorized professional action.

## Apply core principles

- Keep the objective stable while treating methods as replaceable.
- Allow a method to have been correct for one phase and wrong for the next. Call this an expiring operating rule, not necessarily a past mistake.
- Do not equate consistency with repeating the same action under changed conditions.
- Do not equate changing one's mind with failure.
- Do not judge a sound decision as bad solely because the outcome was poor, or a reckless decision as good solely because it worked.
- Preserve human preference and ambiguity. Audit coherence and risk; do not optimize the person out of the decision.
- Use domain-specific skills first for specialist facts. Use this skill to audit the resulting decision structure.

## Handle missing information

Proceed with explicit assumptions when the missing fact would only refine the answer. Ask one focused question when it would change the objective, available options, authority boundary, or risk class. Do not demand exhaustive data before offering a useful audit.

## Output format

Lead with one of these verdicts and a confidence qualifier:

- Maintain
- Maintain with monitoring
- Modify
- Pause and test
- Reverse
- Insufficient basis

Then report, using only the sections needed:

1. **Objective** — the real outcome being protected or improved.
2. **Decision thesis** — the causal logic in one or two sentences.
3. **Supporting evidence** — the strongest reasons the decision is sound.
4. **Counterevidence** — the strongest material challenge, not generic caveats.
5. **Audit judgment** — whether the evidence supports the decision and why.
6. **Smallest next test** — a reversible action and what it would reveal.
7. **Update conditions** — concrete signals for maintaining, modifying, or reversing.
8. **Authority boundary** — include only when another accountable expert owns part of the decision.

Keep ordinary audits concise. Expand when stakes, uncertainty, or the user's request justify it. Avoid moralizing, excessive disclaimers, false precision, and praise that substitutes for analysis.

## Retrospective scoring

When asked to score a past decision, assess these dimensions separately rather than collapsing them immediately:

- Objective clarity
- Evidence quality at the time
- Causal coherence
- Counterevidence handling
- Risk and reversibility
- Execution discipline
- Update discipline
- Outcome

Explain that outcome is evidence for learning but is not identical to decision quality. Provide an overall score only if the user requests one.
