# Update Ryuta Market Outlook

Support Ryuta Sugimoto's judgment; do not replace it. Preserve his original practical wording and decision log unless he explicitly requests a rewrite.

## Core principle

Treat the task as a **regime decision**, not a prediction of the next market tick. Change the published outlook only when an important event or durable regime change alters the risk-reward balance. Keep routine monitoring separate from low-frequency, decision-oriented outlook updates.

Build the judgment from facts already observable in public data. Do not forecast an unknowable event such as the exact end of a war, pandemic, or correction. Accumulate current facts until they imply a direction. Treat this as the central reproducibility rule: **do not imagine the future; process the present correctly**.

Use this loop:

1. Identify what changed since the last view.
2. Build the causal chain from the change to asset prices.
3. Test the chain against confirming and contradicting evidence.
4. Assign the regime.
5. Translate the regime into portfolio weights.
6. State invalidation conditions and monitoring items.
7. Record the change as conclusion, reason, and action.

## Time-horizon discipline

- Separate the long-term regime from a short-term pullback or rally.
- Do not change the long-term allocation because of a weekly data point when the dominant liquidity or policy regime is intact.
- Allow a short-term view to be wrong without automatically invalidating the long-term thesis.
- Distinguish price correction from time correction.
- Prefer waiting in neutral when neither upside nor downside has a clear edge. A rising index alone is not a reason to chase.

## Evidence priority

Check evidence in this order. Do not let a lower-priority short-term move override a stronger regime signal without explaining why.

### 0. Data quality, timing, and observability

- Prefer the newest data that describes the current regime over older quarterly or monthly figures distorted by temporary events.
- Check whether official releases are missing, delayed, revised, or politically disrupted. Do not treat an estimate as equivalent to an observed release.
- When core employment or inflation data are unavailable, lower confidence, avoid a forced regime change, and identify the exact release needed.
- Separate a headline that merely raises volatility from a fact that changes the causal chain.

### 1. Inflation and supply regime

- Check war, sanctions, shipping disruption, and damage to energy or material supply.
- Check oil and other key input prices, including whether normalization is structurally delayed.
- Trace the chain explicitly: event → supply or demand → inflation → central-bank room → discount rates → risk assets.
- Distinguish a temporary price spike from persistent capacity loss or high-price persistence.

### 2. Monetary policy, liquidity, and sovereign rates

- Check actual and expected Fed policy, especially rate cuts versus renewed hike risk.
- Check US 10-year and Japan 10-year yields, not only policy rates.
- Evaluate QT, government bond supply, fiscal deficits, buyer scarcity, interest expense, and term premium.
- Treat higher-for-longer rates and rising term premium as independent valuation pressure even when growth is intact.
- Do not treat a policy-rate hike as bearish by itself. Rate hikes often occur during economic expansion and can confirm that growth is strong.
- Separate **growth-driven tightening** from **inflation- or term-premium-driven long-yield stress**. The latter is the more important equity risk because it directly compresses valuation and raises financing costs.
- Use the US 10-year yield as the decisive condition around an S&P 500 breakout. If the yield is moving toward 5%, keep the bearish regime even when economic activity and the index remain firm.
- Identify whether a yield decline is positive disinflation or a **bad yield decline** caused by deteriorating growth.
- Check central-bank independence and political pressure when they can change the reaction function. Treat a changed reaction function as a regime variable, not political commentary.

### 2A. Market plumbing and cross-asset stability

- During stress, check dollar funding, government bonds, gold, foreign exchange, credit, and VIX before focusing only on equity prices.
- Treat abnormal simultaneous selling of safe assets and risk assets as a liquidity problem.
- Look for normalization in cross-asset correlations as evidence that market plumbing is healing.
- Use chart breaks as early warnings only when a fundamental or liquidity explanation exists. Charts can lead the facts, but do not replace them.

### 3. Earnings and AI investment cycle

- Check GAFAM and other major-company earnings and guidance.
- Check whether hyperscaler AI capital expenditure continues and whether it reaches revenue and profit.
- Separate durable earnings support from price momentum alone.
- Allow a profitable bubble to continue when fundamentals and capital spending still confirm it, while retaining explicit exit signals.
- Check how AI investment is financed. Repeated large debt issuance, long-dated borrowing, and a required return above plausible AI profitability can mark a late-cycle shift even while headline investment remains high.
- Distinguish technological importance from investment profitability. An innovation can be real while the equity cycle is ending.

### 4. Semiconductors, memory, and market internals

- Monitor NASDAQ 100, SOX, Micron, and SK hynix as current high-signal indicators.
- Check memory pricing, earnings expectations, volatility, breadth, and leadership concentration.
- Treat a large listing or financing event such as SK hynix's Nasdaq listing as a possible late-cycle or high-price financing signal, not proof by itself.
- Give more weight to a cluster of deterioration—rising volatility, memory weakness, stretched expectations, narrow leadership—than to one red day.
- Treat semiconductor and memory volatility as a source of market difficulty, not automatic evidence that the whole economy is weakening. Compare chip instability with broader earnings, employment, credit, and cyclical activity before changing the macro regime.

### 5. Valuation, positioning, and price confirmation

- Check whether the market is confirming or rejecting the fundamental thesis.
- Use valuation and positioning as risk multipliers, not standalone timing tools.
- Distinguish healthy correction, selection phase, and genuine regime break.
- Treat a decisive S&P 500 break above its prior high as a valid reason to consider increasing equity exposure. Require confirmation from the US 10-year yield before acting.
- If the S&P 500 breaks out while long yields are stable or falling and the economy remains sound, consider moving from 30% toward 40% rather than waiting for every semiconductor concern to disappear.
- If the S&P 500 breaks out while the US 10-year yield is advancing toward 5%, treat the breakout as insufficient and maintain bearish 30% exposure.
- Examine the market's reaction to news, not only the news itself. For example, favorable inflation data followed by a sharp NASDAQ decline can reveal exhausted positioning or an unwind.
- Check carry-trade and currency-unwind risk when yen funding and large US technology positions appear linked.
- Evaluate returns in yen for a Japan-based investor. Quantify the portfolio effect of a plausible FX move before increasing unhedged foreign assets.
- Treat persistently high intraday volatility and alternating sharp rallies and declines as instability, not a healthy uptrend. When direction is unclear and volatility is high, reduce exposure.

### 6. Credit and household stress

- Check bankruptcies, loan delinquencies, regional-bank losses, auto and subprime credit, and other evidence of household or corporate funding stress.
- Treat a cluster of credit deterioration as confirmation that easing or QT termination may reflect weakness rather than a clean bullish liquidity impulse.
- Compare the risk-reward of equities with hedged developed-market bonds. A lower expected return can still be preferable when falling growth and yields improve bond convexity.

## Regime decision

Start from a conventional 60/40 portfolio as the reference point, then adjust total equity exposure within a hard 20%–70% operating range. Treat the levels below as the default regime map, not a loose range.

| Regime | Equity guide | Typical interpretation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Most bearish | 20% | Reserve for an exceptional crisis or unusually high-conviction downside regime. Do not use this level routinely. |
| Bearish | 30% | Use as the normal defensive equity level. This is the current level as of 2026-07-14. |
| Neutral | about 40% | Evidence is mixed or the previous thesis has weakened without fully reversing. |
| Bullish | about 50–60% | Earnings, liquidity, or policy conditions support risk-taking with normal defenses. |
| Strongest bullish | about 70% | Several high-priority signals align and upside participation is worth elevated exposure. |

Keep the approach long-centered. Use cash and hedged developed-market bonds to reduce volatility. Do not infer leverage, short positions, or a new asset class unless Ryuta explicitly introduces it.

Never recommend 20% merely because several bearish indicators align. Stay at 30% unless the evidence shows an extraordinary systemic, liquidity, inflation, credit, or recession shock comparable to the rarest defensive periods in the record. When considering 20%, explicitly state why 30% is no longer sufficient and identify the exceptional trigger.

## Confirmation checklist

Before recommending a change, answer:

- What changed since the previous published view?
- Is it a durable regime change or short-term noise?
- What is the primary causal chain?
- Which high-priority signals confirm it?
- Which evidence contradicts it?
- Has price action already discounted the change?
- What would prove the new view wrong?
- Does the proposed allocation match the regime and remain internally consistent?
- Is NASDAQ 100 added, retained, reduced, or removed for a stated reason?
- Are bonds appropriate given the direction of yields and term premium?
- Is cash serving a clear volatility and optionality function?
- Is the evidence coming from observable facts or from an imagined future event?
- Are long-term and short-term conclusions being mixed?
- Is the market reaction confirming the headline, or contradicting it?
- Are cross-asset correlations normal, or is liquidity stress distorting them?
- What does the allocation look like in yen after currency risk?
- Are the key data current, officially observed, and available, or stale, estimated, delayed, or missing?
- Does easing represent healthy normalization, politically forced stimulus, or a response to credit and recession stress?
- Is AI investment funded by sustainable cash flow or increasingly by debt with an uncertain return?
- Has the S&P 500 decisively exceeded its prior high, and is that move holding?
- Is the US 10-year yield validating the breakout, or moving toward the 5% danger zone?
- Is a proposed bearish change based on actual economic deterioration, or only on semiconductor volatility?
- If rate hikes are expected, are they a normal consequence of strong growth or a response to uncontrolled inflation and rising long yields?

If the evidence is insufficient, recommend retaining the current regime and list the missing confirmation. Do not manufacture an update merely because new data arrived.

## Invalidation and accountability

State observable invalidation conditions, preferably tied to the causal chain. Examples include normalization of oil and supply, falling inflation, a decisive shift in Fed expectations, reversal in long yields or term premium, renewed AI capex and earnings breadth, or stabilization in memory leadership.

When confirmation arrives gradually, scale exposure in deliberate increments such as 10 percentage points. When a core causal chain breaks or a liquidity unwind appears, allow a decisive reduction rather than insisting on symmetry between entering and exiting.

When a prior view is wrong, say so plainly, correct it, change the portfolio, and retain the history. Do not rewrite the record to make the transition appear smoother.
